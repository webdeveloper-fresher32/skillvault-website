## 1. Concept

Aptitude exams do not test grammar the way a language classroom does. They test whether you can catch **one specific, well-known rule violation** hidden inside an otherwise fluent-sounding sentence, in the 30–60 seconds you have before moving to the next question. The five rule families that show up again and again are:

- **Subject-verb agreement (SVA):** the verb must match its subject in number (singular subject → singular verb, plural subject → plural verb). The trap is almost never the obvious case — it's when a **phrase sits between the subject and the verb** and your eye locks onto the wrong noun. In "The **list** of items **is** long," the subject is "list" (singular), not "items" (the plural noun sitting right next to the verb). Collective nouns — team, committee, jury, family, audience — are a special case: when the group is acting **as one unit**, they take a **singular** verb ("The team **is** practicing for the finals"), even though they refer to many people.
- **Tense consistency:** when a sentence describes two actions that are logically linked in time, both verbs must sit in tenses that agree with that relationship. A common trap is a **past-tense frame with a present-tense verb dropped in by mistake** ("She was cooking dinner when the phone **rings**" — the ringing happened in the past too, so it must be "**rang**").
- **Articles (a / an / the):** the choice between "a" and "an" is governed by **sound, not spelling**. Use "an" before a **vowel sound**, "a" before a **consonant sound** — regardless of which letter the word starts with. "An hour" (the "h" is silent, so the word starts with the vowel sound "our"), but "a university" (spelled with a vowel, but pronounced "yoo-niversity," a consonant "y" sound) and "a one-rupee coin" (pronounced "won," a consonant sound). "The" is used for something specific or already known to both speaker and listener.
- **Prepositions commonly tested:** many words in English pair with one specific, fixed preposition, and exams test the pairing that's easy to get wrong by analogy with a different (often American or informal) usage — "different **from**" (not "different than"), "married **to**" (not "married with"), "good **at**" (not "good in"), "depend **on**," "arrived **at**" a small point/building vs "arrived **in**" a city or country.
- **Parallelism:** when a sentence lists two or more items joined by "and," "or," or a correlative pair like "not only...but also," every item in that list must share the **same grammatical form** — all nouns, all gerunds ("-ing"), or all infinitives ("to + verb"). A list that mixes forms ("she likes reading, writing, and **to swim**") reads as broken even though each individual item is a valid English phrase.

These five rules cover the overwhelming majority of grammar-based questions in competitive aptitude exams. The skill being tested is not memorizing terminology — it's **noticing the mismatch on a fast read**, which is exactly what Section 2 trains.

## 2. Shortcut / Trick

For each rule family, use one fast, mechanical check instead of reasoning through grammar theory from scratch:

- **SVA — find the real subject by deleting the middle phrase.** Mentally cross out any prepositional phrase or clause sitting between the subject and the verb, then check agreement on what's left. "The **basket** of apples (delete this) **is** on the table" — "basket is," not "apples is/are." For collective nouns, ask: "are they acting as one single block, or as separate individuals?" — acting as one block takes a singular verb.
- **Tense consistency — find the "anchor" tense first.** Identify which tense the sentence is fundamentally set in (usually established by the first main verb), then check every other verb in the sentence agrees with that same time-frame, unless a time-marker word (e.g., "already," "since," "tomorrow") explicitly justifies a shift.
- **Articles — say the next word out loud (in your head) and listen for the first sound, not the first letter.** If what you hear at the start is a vowel sound ("ow," "eh," "ee," "aw," "yoo" is NOT a vowel sound — it's a consonant glide), use "an." Otherwise use "a."
- **Prepositions — memorize the fixed pairing, don't translate literally from how the sentence "feels."** These pairings are fixed vocabulary facts (like spelling), not logic puzzles, so the fastest method is direct recall of the short list of commonly tested pairs above, rather than re-deriving them each time.
- **Parallelism — strip the list down to just the connecting words and check if they all "plug in" the same way.** Read the sentence's frame with each list item substituted one at a time: "she likes **reading**" / "she likes **writing**" / "she likes **to swim**" — the third one breaks the pattern the first two established. Whichever form the first item uses, force every later item into that same form.

## 3. Worked Examples

### (a) Subject-verb agreement with a collective noun

**Sentence:** "The team of players **are** practicing hard for the finals."

**Analysis:** Delete the prepositional phrase "of players" — the true subject is "**team**," a collective noun referring to the group as a single practicing unit, not to the players as separate individuals doing different things. A singular collective noun acting as one unit takes a singular verb.

**Corrected:** "The team of players **is** practicing hard for the finals."

### (b) Correct preposition usage

**Sentence:** "This result is different **than** the one we expected."

**Analysis:** "Different" pairs with the fixed preposition "**from**" in standard usage tested in competitive exams — "different than" is a common but non-standard substitution that exams mark as an error.

**Corrected:** "This result is different **from** the one we expected."

### (c) Parallelism error in a list

**Sentence:** "The manager asked the staff to arrive early, to complete their tasks, and **submitting** the report by evening."

**Analysis:** The list is joined by "and" and describes three things the manager asked the staff to do. The first two items use the infinitive form ("to arrive," "to complete"); the third item switches to a gerund ("submitting"), breaking the parallel structure the first two items established.

**Corrected:** "The manager asked the staff to arrive early, to complete their tasks, and **to submit** the report by evening."

## 4. Timed Practice Set

1. Find and correct the error: "She was cooking dinner when the phone rings." (45 sec)
2. Find and correct the error: "He is an European citizen." (40 sec)
3. Find and correct the error: "He is good in mathematics." (40 sec)
4. Find and correct the error: "Neither of the answers are correct." (45 sec)
5. Find and correct the error: "The course covers grammar, vocabulary, and how to write essays." (50 sec)
6. Find and correct the error: "I need an one-rupee coin." (40 sec)
7. Find and correct the error: "The committee have submitted the report." (45 sec)
8. Find and correct the error: "She was married with a doctor last year." (40 sec)

### Answer Key

1. **"...when the phone rang."** The sentence is anchored in the past ("was cooking"); the second verb must stay in the same past time-frame, not shift to present tense "rings."
2. **"He is a European citizen."** "European" is spelled with a vowel but starts with the consonant sound "yoo," so it takes "a," not "an."
3. **"He is good at mathematics."** "Good" pairs with the fixed preposition "at," not "in."
4. **"Neither of the answers is correct."** "Neither" is a singular subject (deleting "of the answers" leaves "Neither is correct"), so it takes a singular verb, even though "answers" is plural.
5. **"...grammar, vocabulary, and essay writing."** The first two list items are plain nouns; the third must match that noun form instead of switching to an infinitive phrase ("how to write essays").
6. **"I need a one-rupee coin."** "One" is pronounced "won," a consonant sound, so it takes "a," not "an."
7. **"The committee has submitted the report."** "Committee" is a collective noun acting as a single unit submitting one report, so it takes the singular verb "has," not "have."
8. **"She was married to a doctor last year."** "Married" pairs with the fixed preposition "to," not "with."
