## 1. Concept

**Non-verbal reasoning** questions replace words and numbers with shapes, symbols, and figures. There is no vocabulary or arithmetic to know — the entire task is to spot the exact rule that connects a set of figures and apply it correctly. The recurring question families are:

- **Figure series (rotation).** A sequence of figures where one visual element (an arrow, a shaded region, a marked corner) turns by a fixed angle — usually 45°, 90°, or 180° — in a fixed direction (clockwise or anticlockwise) from one figure to the next. You must find the angle and direction from the given figures, then predict what comes next.
- **Figure series (shape addition / counting).** A sequence where a simple element (a dot, a small shape, a line) is added, removed, or accumulated according to a pattern (e.g., "one more line each step," "sides increase by one each step") as you move through the series.
- **Mirror image.** Given one figure, you must identify its reflection across a vertical or horizontal axis (as if held up to a mirror). A mirror flip reverses left-right (across a vertical mirror line) or up-down (across a horizontal mirror line) — but **never both at once**. A figure that has been flipped both ways is not a mirror image; it is the same as a 180° rotation, and this confusion is the single most common wrong-answer trap in this family.
- **Figure classification (odd one out).** Given a set of figures (usually five), four share a common structural property (e.g., "all have exactly one enclosed region," "all are made of exactly 4 line segments," "all have an odd number of sides") and one does not. The task is to find the property the majority share, not to look for surface similarity (like "which two look most alike").
- **Embedded figures.** A small, simple target figure is hidden inside a larger, more cluttered figure, disguised by extra lines, rotation, or overlap. The task is to trace whether the target's exact shape (same set of line segments, same relative angles and lengths) can be found as a sub-part of the complex figure.

The one idea underlying all five families: **non-verbal reasoning tests whether you can isolate the *one* rule or structural property that governs a set of figures, ignoring everything about the figures that is irrelevant to that rule** — size, exact position on the page, and orientation are frequently red herrings; count, angle, and connectivity are usually what actually matters.

## 2. Shortcut / Trick

- **For rotation series:** Pick one clearly identifiable marked feature (an arrowhead, a shaded cell, a dot) and track *only* that feature's position across the given figures, ignoring the rest of the figure if it stays constant. Express its movement as "moves N positions clockwise/anticlockwise per step" (e.g., using clock positions or grid-cell labels). Once the rule is fixed from at least two consecutive steps, apply it one more time to predict the next figure.
- **For shape addition/counting series:** Count the relevant element (lines, dots, sides) in each given figure and write the counts as a plain number sequence (e.g., 3, 4, 5, 6). Solve it exactly like a numeric series (Phase 7) — find the common difference or multiplier — then translate the next number back into a figure.
- **For mirror image:** Decide the mirror axis (usually stated: "vertical mirror" or "left side of the given figure"). Flip **only** the dimension the axis specifies — left becomes right (vertical mirror) while up/down positions of every part stay exactly where they were. If an answer option has swapped both left-right and up-down, it's a rotation trap, not a mirror image — eliminate it immediately.
- **For classification (odd one out):** Never eyeball "which one looks different" first — that leads straight to surface traps (color, size, small stray marks). Instead, list a candidate property (side count, line count, number of enclosed regions, presence/absence of curves) and test it against all five figures. If four match and one doesn't, that's your answer; if the property doesn't cleanly separate 4-vs-1, try a different property.
- **For embedded figures:** Take the target figure and note its "skeleton" — how many line segments it has and how they connect (which endpoints touch which). Then scan the complex figure for that exact skeleton, allowing rotation but not allowing the segments to be a different length ratio or connect at different points. A common trap is a set of lines in the complex figure that "looks like" the target at a glance but has one connection in the wrong place — trace it, don't eyeball it.

## 3. Worked Examples

### (a) Rotating-shape series — identify the next figure

Each figure below is a 2×2 grid (a square split into 4 equal quadrants: top-left, top-right, bottom-right, bottom-left). Exactly one quadrant is shaded (`#`); the other three are empty (`.`).

```
Figure 1:      Figure 2:      Figure 3:      Figure 4:
 # .            . #            . .            ? ?
 . .            . .            . #            ? ?
```

**Tracking the shaded quadrant only:** Figure 1 has it top-left. Figure 2 has it top-right. Figure 3 has it bottom-right. Reading top-left → top-right → bottom-right, the shaded quadrant is moving one position **clockwise** around the square each step (top-left → top-right → bottom-right → bottom-left → back to top-left).

**Applying the rule once more:** after bottom-right (Figure 3), one more clockwise step lands on **bottom-left**.

```
Figure 4:
 . .
 # .
```

**Answer:** Figure 4 has the shaded quadrant in the bottom-left cell, all others empty.

### (b) Mirror-image reasoning

**Given figure:** an arrow drawn on a 5×5 grid, shaft running from the bottom-left corner to the top-right corner (pointing "northeast," ↗), with a small circle (`o`) attached at its tail end (bottom-left) and the arrowhead at the top-right end.

```
Given:
o . . . .
. . . . .
. . . . .
. . . . .
. . . . ↗
```
(reading the shaft as the diagonal from bottom-left to top-right; `↗` marks the arrowhead corner, `o` marks the tail corner)

**Question:** which of these two options is the correct **mirror image** across a **vertical** mirror line (i.e., held up to a mirror standing on the right or left side of the figure, which swaps left and right but keeps up and down unchanged)?

- **Option X:** arrowhead at top-left, tail circle at bottom-right — shaft runs from bottom-right to top-left (↖ direction).
- **Option Y:** arrowhead at bottom-left, tail circle at top-right — shaft runs from top-right to bottom-left (↙ direction).

**Reasoning:** a vertical mirror swaps *only* left and right; whatever was at the top stays at the top, and whatever was at the bottom stays at the bottom. In the given figure, the arrowhead (originally top-right) is at the **top**, and the tail circle (originally bottom-left) is at the **bottom**. After a left-right flip, the arrowhead should still be at the **top** (just moved from the right side to the left side), and the tail circle should still be at the **bottom** (moved from the left side to the right side).

- Option X keeps the arrowhead at the top (now top-left) and the tail circle at the bottom (now bottom-right) — only left-right has changed, top/bottom are preserved. This matches a vertical mirror flip exactly.
- Option Y has swapped the arrowhead down to the bottom and the tail circle up to the top — that is an up/down swap as well, which does not happen in a vertical mirror. Option Y is what you'd get from rotating the figure 180°, not from mirroring it — this is the classic "rotation disguised as a mirror image" trap.

**Answer: Option X** is the correct mirror image.

### (c) Counting shapes in a figure description

**Figure:** A square PQRS (corners labeled P top-left, Q top-right, R bottom-right, S bottom-left) with **both diagonals** drawn — diagonal PR and diagonal QS — crossing at the center point O.

```
P--------Q
|\      /|
| \    / |
|  \  /  |
|   \/   |
|   /\   |
|  /  \  |
| /    \ |
|/      \|
S--------R
```

**Question:** how many triangles are there in total in this figure?

**Counting systematically — smallest first, then build up:**

- **Smallest triangles** (each bounded by one half of each diagonal plus one full side of the square): POQ (using side PQ), QOR (using side QR), ROS (using side RS), SOP (using side SP). That's **4** small triangles.
- **Larger triangles** (each formed by one full diagonal plus two full sides of the square): PQR (side PQ + side QR + diagonal PR), QRS (side QR + side RS + diagonal QS), RSP (side RS + side SP + diagonal PR), SPQ (side SP + side PQ + diagonal QS). That's **4** larger triangles.
- No other combination of the drawn line segments closes into a triangle (any set of three lines not sharing this pattern either doesn't close up or repeats one already counted).

**Answer: 8 triangles** in total (4 small + 4 large).

## 4. Timed Practice Set

1. A series of figures shows a single arrow inside a square, pointing up (↑) in figure 1, pointing right (→) in figure 2, and pointing down (↓) in figure 3. Following the same rotation rule, which direction does the arrow point in figure 4? (30 sec)
2. A figure is a capital letter "R" drawn normally. Which of these is its correct mirror image across a vertical mirror: (i) an "R" with its vertical stroke on the right and the loop/leg opening to the left, or (ii) an upside-down "R"? (35 sec)
3. Five figures are described: (i) an equilateral triangle (all 3 sides equal), (ii) a square (all 4 sides equal), (iii) a rectangle with length different from breadth, (iv) a regular pentagon (all 5 sides equal), (v) a rhombus (all 4 sides equal). Four of these figures have all their sides equal to each other; one does not. Which one is the odd one out? (30 sec)
4. A series of figures shows a hexagon with 0 dots inside it, then 2 dots, then 4 dots, then 6 dots. Following the same pattern, how many dots should the 5th figure have? (30 sec)
5. A complex figure is described as a large triangle with a smaller triangle drawn upside-down and centered exactly inside it, touching the midpoint of each side (so the large triangle is divided into 4 smaller triangular regions: the central inverted one and three corner ones, with no other lines drawn). How many triangles are there in total in this complete figure? (60 sec)
6. A figure shows a small target shape: two line segments joined at a right angle, like the letter "L" (one vertical segment, one horizontal segment of equal length, meeting at the bottom). It is embedded (possibly rotated) inside a larger complex figure made of a square with both of its diagonals drawn. Is the "L" shape (a plain right angle formed by two segments of equal length meeting at one point, with nothing else attached) found anywhere among the segments of the square-with-diagonals figure? (45 sec)

### Answer Key

1. **Left (←).** Tracking the arrow's direction only: up → right → down is a 90° clockwise turn each step (↑ → → → ↓). One more 90° clockwise turn from down (↓) lands on left (←).
2. **(i).** A vertical mirror swaps only left and right, keeping top and bottom fixed — so the "R"'s vertical stroke (originally on the left) moves to the right, and its loop/leg (originally opening to the right) now opens to the left, while the letter stays upright. Option (ii), the upside-down "R," swaps top and bottom as well — that is a 180° rotation, not a mirror image, and is the classic trap.
3. **The rectangle (iii).** Checking the "all sides equal" property directly: equilateral triangle — all 3 sides equal (yes); square — all 4 sides equal (yes); rectangle — length ≠ breadth, so only opposite sides match, not all four (no); regular pentagon — all 5 sides equal (yes); rhombus — all 4 sides equal (yes). Four figures satisfy "all sides equal," and the rectangle is the only one that doesn't.
4. **8 dots.** Counting the dot pattern as a plain number series: 0, 2, 4, 6 — each figure adds exactly 2 dots to the previous one. The 5th figure continues the same +2 rule: 6 + 2 = 8.
5. **5 triangles.** The complete figure has exactly 4 small triangular regions (the central inverted one plus the 3 corner ones) and no other lines are drawn, so no other partial combination of segments closes into a triangle — any two adjacent regions taken together form a four-sided trapezoid, not a triangle. Adding the 1 large outer triangle (the original big triangle's own boundary) gives 4 (small regions) + 1 (large triangle) = **5** triangles total.
6. **No.** The target "L" shape is a plain right angle made of exactly two segments of equal length meeting at one point, with no other segment touching that vertex and no other line crossing either segment. In the square-with-diagonals figure, every line segment (each side, each half-diagonal) that meets another segment at a corner or at the center does so alongside additional lines at the same point (e.g., at each corner of the square, one side meets one diagonal, but a second side also touches that same corner, so three segments meet there, not two; at the center O, all four diagonal-halves meet, not two). Since no vertex in the square-with-diagonals figure has exactly two segments of equal length meeting with nothing else attached, the plain "L" cannot be traced anywhere in it — it is not embedded in this figure.
