## 1. Concept

Two closely related question types build on the grammar rules from the previous lesson:

- **Error-spotting** gives you a sentence split into four labeled parts — (A), (B), (C), (D) — plus a fifth option, "(E) No error." You must identify which single part (if any) contains a grammatical mistake.
- **Sentence correction** gives you a full sentence with one phrase underlined, followed by four or five rephrasings of that underlined portion (one of which is usually "no change"). You must pick the rephrasing that fixes the sentence without introducing a new problem.

Both formats reuse the rules from Lesson 1 (agreement, tense, articles, prepositions, parallelism), but they also lean heavily on three additional trap categories that are less about a single word and more about the sentence's overall logic:

- **Redundancy:** saying the same thing twice using different words, where one of the two is unnecessary. "**Return back**" is redundant because "return" already means "go back" — the word "back" adds nothing. Other classics: "**each and every**," "**past history**," "**repeat again**," "**free gift**."
- **Misplaced (dangling) modifiers:** an introductory descriptive phrase must be followed immediately by the noun it actually describes. If the wrong noun follows it, the sentence accidentally claims that noun performed the action in the modifier. "**Walking along the beach, the sunset looked beautiful**" literally claims the sunset was walking — it wasn't; a person was.
- **Faulty comparison:** you can only logically compare two things of the **same kind** — a price to a price, a population to a population, a climate to a climate. "The price of gold is higher than **silver**" illegally compares "the price of gold" (a price) to "silver" (a metal), instead of to "**the price of** silver."

These categories are "less mechanical" than Lesson 1's rules because catching them requires reading for **logical sense**, not just checking word-level agreement — which is exactly why exams favor them for testing genuine reading carefulness under time pressure.

## 2. Shortcut / Trick

**The underline-and-scan method**, used in this fixed order every time:

1. **Read the whole sentence once, quickly, for overall meaning.** Don't stop at the underlined/split portion yet — you need the full context to judge tense, logic, and comparison.
2. **Scan the sentence against five checkpoints, in this order, stopping at the first one that fires:**
   - Subject-verb agreement (delete the middle phrase, check the real subject)
   - Tense consistency (find the anchor tense, check every verb against it)
   - Redundancy (any pair of words that says the same thing twice?)
   - Misplaced modifier (does the noun right after an introductory phrase actually match what the phrase describes?)
   - Faulty comparison (are the two things being compared actually the same kind of thing?)
3. **Only mark "No error" after actively failing to find a fault on all five checkpoints** — never as a first instinct, since the option is deliberately placed to catch people who scan too quickly to check every category.

Two targeted techniques for the trickiest categories:

- **For a suspected misplaced modifier: cover the modifying phrase and read only what's left.** If "the sunset looked beautiful" is the leftover core sentence, then ask "does the covered-up phrase logically describe THIS subject?" "Walking along the beach" cannot logically describe "the sunset" — the mismatch is now obvious with the noise removed.
- **For a suspected faulty comparison: mentally insert the missing "that of" / "those of" and see if the comparison suddenly becomes logical.** "The price of gold is higher than [that of] silver" — if inserting the missing phrase fixes the logic, the original sentence was missing it and that's the error.
- **For sentence-correction (multiple-choice rephrasing) questions specifically, eliminate options in this order:** first discard any option that still contains the original error; next discard any option that fixes the original error but introduces a **new** grammar mistake; next discard any option that changes the sentence's original meaning; whatever survives this three-pass elimination is the answer.

## 3. Worked Examples

### (a) Spot the error in an underlined sentence

**Sentence:** "*Walking along the beach*, the sunset looked more beautiful than any other evening."

**Analysis:** Cover the underlined introductory phrase and read the core sentence: "the sunset looked more beautiful than any other evening." Now ask whether "walking along the beach" logically describes "the sunset" — it cannot, since a sunset does not walk. The phrase is meant to describe a person (implied but never named) who was walking and observing the sunset. This is a classic dangling/misplaced modifier.

**Corrected:** "Walking along the beach, **she** found the sunset more beautiful than any other evening." (The modifier now correctly attaches to the person doing the walking.)

### (b) Faulty comparison correction

**Sentence:** "The climate of Bangalore is more pleasant than Chennai."

**Analysis:** The sentence compares "the climate of Bangalore" (a quality) directly to "Chennai" (a city) — two different kinds of things. Mentally insert the missing phrase: "...more pleasant than **that of** Chennai" — now the comparison is climate-to-climate, and the sentence becomes logical.

**Corrected:** "The climate of Bangalore is more pleasant than **that of** Chennai."

### (c) Redundancy elimination

**Sentence:** "Please return back the borrowed books to the library by Friday."

**Analysis:** Delete the suspected redundant word and check whether the meaning changes: "Please return the borrowed books to the library by Friday" means exactly the same thing. Since deleting "back" changes nothing, "back" was redundant — "return" already carries the meaning of "back."

**Corrected:** "Please return the borrowed books to the library by Friday."

## 4. Timed Practice Set

Each sentence below is split into four labeled parts. Identify the part containing the error, or mark (E) if there is no error.

1. (A) Each of the boys / (B) have submitted / (C) their assignment / (D) on time. (E) No error (45 sec)
2. (A) The train reached the / (B) station before the / (C) scheduled time and / (D) all the passengers got down safely. (E) No error (45 sec)
3. (A) Raj is one of the / (B) best players who / (C) has ever played / (D) for the national team. (E) No error (50 sec)
4. (A) He returned back / (B) home late at night / (C) after finishing / (D) all his work. (E) No error (40 sec)
5. (A) Walking along the beach, / (B) the sunset looked / (C) more beautiful than / (D) any other evening. (E) No error (50 sec)
6. (A) The price of gold / (B) is much higher / (C) than silver / (D) in the current market. (E) No error (50 sec)
7. (A) She not only / (B) completed her project / (C) but also she submitted / (D) it before the deadline. (E) No error (50 sec)
8. (A) Each and every student / (B) in the class / (C) participated actively in / (D) the debate competition. (E) No error (40 sec)

### Answer Key

1. **(B).** The subject is "Each" (singular), not "boys" — deleting "of the boys" leaves "Each have submitted," which should be "Each **has** submitted."
2. **(E).** No error — the subject "the train" agrees with "reached," the tense is consistent throughout, and "all the passengers got down" agrees in number. Nothing to fix.
3. **(C).** "Who" refers back to "players" (plural), not to "Raj" alone, so the relative-clause verb must be plural: "who **have** ever played," not "has."
4. **(A).** "Returned back" is redundant — "returned" already means "went back," so "back" adds nothing and should be dropped.
5. **(B).** Covering the modifier "Walking along the beach" and reading "the sunset looked..." shows the sunset cannot be the one walking — a dangling modifier; the subject following the phrase should be a person, not "the sunset."
6. **(C).** "The price of gold" (a price) is being compared directly to "silver" (a metal) instead of to "the price of silver" — insert "that of": "than **that of** silver."
7. **(C).** The correlative pair "not only...but also" requires parallel structure without repeating the subject: "but also **submitted** it," not "but also she submitted."
8. **(A).** "Each and every" is redundant — "each" and "every" already mean the same thing individually, so only one of the two is needed ("**Every** student..." or "**Each** student...").
