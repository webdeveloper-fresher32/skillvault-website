## 1. Concept

Vocabulary questions test three related but distinct skills:

- **Synonym questions:** pick the word closest in meaning to a given word.
- **Antonym questions:** pick the word most nearly opposite in meaning to a given word.
- **Idiom/phrase questions:** identify the meaning of a fixed expression whose overall meaning cannot be worked out from its individual words (e.g., "spill the beans" has nothing to do with beans).

You will not know every word tested on exam day — the vocabulary pool is too large to fully memorize. What is learnable and reliable under time pressure is a **root-word technique**: most English words are built from Latin or Greek roots, prefixes, and suffixes with stable meanings, and recognizing a piece of an unfamiliar word narrows down its meaning even when the whole word is new to you. For example, the prefix **"bene-"** (Latin, "good/well") appears in "benevolent" (wishing others well), "benefit" (a good result), and "benefactor" (someone who does good/gives help) — once you know "bene-" means "good," an unfamiliar word like "benediction" is guessable as "a good saying/blessing" even without having memorized it before.

**Common confusable word pairs** are words that look or sound similar but differ in meaning, and exams exploit this similarity to build wrong options:

- **Affect (verb, "to influence") vs. Effect (noun, "a result")** — "The weather can **affect** your mood" vs. "The **effect** of the weather was a delayed flight."
- **Complement (something that completes/goes well with) vs. Compliment (praise)** — "The scarf **complements** her outfit" vs. "She received a **compliment** on her outfit."
- **Stationary (not moving) vs. Stationery (paper/writing materials)** — the car was **stationary**; she bought **stationery** for the office.
- **Continual (repeated often, with gaps) vs. Continuous (uninterrupted, without any gaps)** — "continual interruptions" (happening again and again) vs. "continuous rain" (never stopping).
- **Eminent (distinguished, well-known) vs. Imminent (about to happen)** — an **eminent** scientist; an **imminent** storm.

**Frequently-tested idioms**, with meaning:

- **"Spill the beans"** — reveal a secret.
- **"Bite the bullet"** — face a difficult situation with courage; do something unpleasant that can no longer be avoided.
- **"Once in a blue moon"** — very rarely.
- **"Beat around the bush"** — avoid saying something directly; delay getting to the point.
- **"A blessing in disguise"** — something that seems bad at first but turns out to be good.
- **"Cut corners"** — do something in the easiest or cheapest way, often sacrificing quality.
- **"Under the weather"** — feeling slightly ill.
- **"Call it a day"** — stop working on something, usually for the rest of the day.

## 2. Shortcut / Trick

**Root-word elimination for synonym/antonym questions**, applied in this order:

1. **Break the unfamiliar word into recognizable pieces** — a prefix (e.g., "anti-," "pre-," "bene-," "mal-"), a root (e.g., "-dict-" = say, "-vor-" = eat, "-phon-" = sound), and a suffix (e.g., "-ous" = full of, "-phile" = loving).
2. **Assign each piece a rough meaning and combine them**, even if the combination feels approximate — an approximate meaning is enough to eliminate options that are clearly unrelated.
3. **Eliminate every option whose core meaning clashes with your root-based guess**, then choose among what's left based on which fits the sentence's degree and tone.

**Avoiding the antonym near-synonym trap:** the most common wrong-option strategy in antonym questions is to include a word that is a **synonym**, not an opposite, of the given word — testing whether you actually check the relationship direction instead of pattern-matching on "this word feels related." The fix: for every option, explicitly ask "same direction or opposite direction?" before picking — never just "does this word feel connected to the given word?"

**For idiom questions:** never translate word-by-word. If a literal, word-by-word reading produces a nonsensical image (beans that spill, a bullet that gets bitten, a bush being beaten), that is the signal you are looking at a fixed idiom whose meaning must be recalled as a whole unit, not decoded from its parts.

## 3. Worked Examples

### (a) Synonym question solved via root-word elimination

**Question:** Choose the word closest in meaning to **MALEDICTION**.
(A) Compliment
(B) Curse
(C) Celebration
(D) Silence

**Analysis:** Break the word down: "mal-" (Latin, "bad") + "-dict-" (Latin, "to say") → literally "a bad saying." Eliminate (A) Compliment — praise is the opposite of "bad saying." Eliminate (C) Celebration and (D) Silence — neither involves speech at all, and the root clearly signals speech ("-dict-"). Only (B) Curse — a spoken wish of harm — matches both pieces of the root: "bad" and "said."

**Answer:** (B) Curse

### (b) Antonym trap — choosing a near-synonym by mistake

**Question:** Choose the word most nearly OPPOSITE in meaning to **FRUGAL**.
(A) Thrifty
(B) Wasteful
(C) Careful
(D) Modest

**Analysis:** "Frugal" means careful and economical with money or resources. A rushed reader may pick (A) "Thrifty" or (C) "Careful" because those words feel "in the same neighborhood" as money-related vocabulary — but both are actually **synonyms** of frugal, not opposites, which is exactly the trap: they are related in topic (spending habits) but point in the *same* direction as the given word, not the opposite direction. (D) "Modest" is unrelated to spending at all. Only (B) "Wasteful" — spending or using resources carelessly, with no regard for economy — points in the exact opposite direction from "frugal."

**Answer:** (B) Wasteful

### (c) Idiom meaning-in-context question

**Sentence:** "After weeks of avoiding the topic, she finally decided to **bite the bullet** and tell her manager about the mistake."

**Question:** What does the underlined idiom mean in this context?
(A) She literally bit down on a bullet.
(B) She faced an unpleasant task that she could no longer avoid.
(C) She celebrated a success.
(D) She asked someone else to handle the problem for her.

**Analysis:** A literal reading (A) is nonsensical in context — nobody is biting an actual bullet in an office setting. The surrounding context ("after weeks of avoiding," "finally decided," "tell her manager about the mistake") signals a difficult, previously-avoided action being confronted, which matches the idiom's standard meaning: facing a difficult or unpleasant situation with courage. (C) and (D) both contradict the context, since neither a celebration nor delegating the problem matches "finally decided... to tell her manager."

**Answer:** (B)

## 4. Timed Practice Set

1. Choose the word closest in meaning to **PHILANTHROPIST**. (A) A person who studies philosophy (B) A person who gives generously to help others (C) A person who dislikes people (D) A person who collects rare books (30 sec)
2. Choose the word closest in meaning to **CARNIVOROUS**. (A) Plant-eating (B) Meat-eating (C) Nocturnal (D) Slow-moving (25 sec)
3. Choose the word most nearly OPPOSITE in meaning to **VERBOSE**. (A) Talkative (B) Concise (C) Wordy (D) Loud (30 sec)
4. Choose the word most nearly OPPOSITE in meaning to **TRANSPARENT**. (A) Clear (B) Obvious (C) Opaque (D) Fragile (25 sec)
5. Fill in the blank with the correctly spelled/used word: "The two designs are so similar that it's hard to tell the ___ between them." (A) Affect (B) Effect (C) Different (D) Difference (25 sec)
6. Fill in the blank: "The scientist was ___ in her field, having published groundbreaking research for over two decades." (A) Imminent (B) Eminent (C) Immanent (D) Emanate (30 sec)
7. What does the idiom "once in a blue moon" mean in the sentence: "We only visit our cousins once in a blue moon, since they live overseas"? (A) Every full moon (B) Very rarely (C) Only at night (D) Very frequently (25 sec)
8. What does the idiom "cut corners" mean in the sentence: "The contractor cut corners on the foundation to finish the project faster and cheaper"? (A) Built the foundation in a square shape (B) Did the work carelessly to save time or money (C) Removed the corners of the building (D) Finished the project exactly on schedule with no compromises (30 sec)

### Answer Key

1. **(B).** "Phil-" (Greek, "loving") + "-anthrop-" (Greek, "human/mankind") → "loving mankind" — a person who gives generously to help others; (A) confuses the root with "philosophy" ("phil-" + "-sophia," a different combination).
2. **(B).** "Carn-" (Latin, "flesh/meat") + "-vor-" (Latin, "to eat") → "meat-eating"; (A) "plant-eating" is the meaning of "herbivorous," a different root ("herb-" = plant), making it a trap for someone who mixes up the two roots.
3. **(B).** "Verbose" means using far more words than necessary; "concise" (brief and to the point) is the direct opposite. (A) and (C) are near-synonyms of "verbose," not opposites — the classic antonym trap.
4. **(C).** "Transparent" means allowing light/information through clearly; "opaque" (not allowing light through, impossible to see through) is the direct opposite. (A) and (B) are near-synonyms of "transparent," not opposites.
5. **(D).** "Difference" (noun, a point of unlikeness) is required after "the," and the sentence needs a noun, not the adjective "different" or the unrelated affect/effect pair — "it's hard to tell the difference between them" is the only grammatically and semantically correct fit.
6. **(B).** "Eminent" means distinguished/well-known in one's field, matching "having published groundbreaking research for over two decades"; "imminent" (about to happen) does not fit a description of an established career.
7. **(B).** "Once in a blue moon" is an idiom meaning "very rarely" — it does not refer to an actual lunar event; the context ("since they live overseas") supports infrequent visits.
8. **(B).** "Cut corners" means to do something in the cheapest or easiest way, usually sacrificing quality — matching "to finish the project faster and cheaper," which implies a compromise on proper building standards.
