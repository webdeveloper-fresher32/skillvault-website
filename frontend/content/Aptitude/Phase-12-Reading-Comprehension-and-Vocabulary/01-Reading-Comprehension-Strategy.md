## 1. Concept

A reading comprehension (RC) passage is followed by a set of questions, and the exam rewards a specific *strategy* for attacking that pair — not just "reading well" in the everyday sense.

- **Read-first strategy:** read the entire passage carefully from start to finish, absorbing detail and structure, then read the questions and go back to locate answers. This produces strong comprehension but is slow — most candidates cannot afford the time it takes on every passage in a timed section.
- **Skim-first strategy:** read the passage quickly once to capture only the **main idea, structure, and tone** (which paragraph does what), without pausing to memorize details, then read each question and go back to the relevant paragraph to dig out the specific answer. This is faster because the first pass is deliberately shallow, and the required precision is added only where a question actually demands it.

Both strategies use the same underlying skill (careful reading), but the skim-first strategy applies that skill selectively — a full careful read on the whole passage, versus a full careful read only on the one or two sentences a given question is actually about.

RC questions fall into four recurring types, and recognizing the type before you search the passage tells you *how* to search:

- **Main idea:** asks what the passage as a whole is about, or what would be the best title/summary. Answered from the overall shape of the passage (opening and closing sentences of each paragraph), never from one buried detail.
- **Inference:** asks what the passage *implies* but never states outright — you must combine two stated facts to reach a conclusion the author didn't spell out in words.
- **Detail (fact-based):** asks about something the passage states explicitly — a number, a name, a cause, a sequence. Answered by locating the exact sentence and matching it word-for-word.
- **Tone/attitude:** asks how the author feels about the subject (critical, neutral, admiring, skeptical) — answered from word choice and phrasing, not from the literal content of any one sentence.

**Time budgeting:** a common working split for a single passage-plus-questions set is roughly 20% of the allotted time on the first skim pass, and the remaining 80% split across the questions in proportion to how many there are — so a 5-minute passage with 5 questions might get about 1 minute to skim and roughly 45–50 seconds per question, leaving a small buffer. The skim pass is an investment: it turns every later question search from "re-read the whole passage" into "jump straight to paragraph 2."

## 2. Shortcut / Trick

**The skim-classify-hunt method**, applied in this fixed order:

1. **Skim once for shape, not detail.** Read only the first sentence of each paragraph (and the last sentence of the final paragraph) at normal speed; do not slow down for names, numbers, or examples. After this pass you should be able to state, in one sentence, "this passage is about X, and it makes the point that Y."
2. **Classify each question before searching.** Read the question stem and decide, before looking at the passage again, which of the four types it is (main idea / inference / detail / tone). The type tells you *where* to look:
   - Main idea → answer from your one-sentence shape summary; do not re-search the passage at all.
   - Detail → the question stem usually contains a distinctive keyword (a name, number, or unusual noun) — scan for that exact keyword and read only the sentence containing it.
   - Inference → find the one or two sentences the question is clearly about, then ask "what must also be true if these sentences are true?" — the answer is never a word-for-word match to the passage; it is one logical step beyond it.
   - Tone → do not hunt for a specific sentence; instead recall the adjectives and verbs the author used throughout (e.g., "surprisingly," "unfortunately," "clearly demonstrates") — these carry the attitude, not the facts.
3. **For inference questions specifically, actively reject any option that is directly stated in the passage** — a directly-stated option is a trap for a detail question, not a valid inference, since an inference by definition is not written in the text.
4. **For tone questions, eliminate extreme-word options first** (e.g., "furious," "ecstatic") unless the passage's language is itself extreme — most passages carry a mild, measured tone, and exams exploit this by offering one wildly overstated option as a distractor.

## 3. Worked Examples

### (a) Main-idea question via the skim strategy

**Passage (skim pass only — first/last sentences):** "Urban rooftop gardens have moved from a niche hobby to a mainstream feature of city planning. ... As more cities adopt supportive policies, rooftop gardens are likely to become a standard, rather than exceptional, part of urban design."

**Question:** What is the main idea of the passage?
(A) Rooftop gardens are difficult to maintain in cities.
(B) Rooftop gardens are shifting from a niche practice to a standard feature of urban planning.
(C) Only wealthy cities can afford rooftop gardens.
(D) Rooftop gardens were invented recently.

**Analysis:** The skim pass alone (first sentence: "moved from a niche hobby to a mainstream feature"; last sentence: "likely to become a standard... part of urban design") already gives the shape of the whole passage without reading a single middle sentence. Option (B) restates that shape exactly; the other three each rely on a supporting detail, not the overall point.

**Answer:** (B)

### (b) Inference question — why literal reading fails

**Passage excerpt:** "The company reported record profits this quarter, yet it announced a hiring freeze and delayed two planned store openings."

**Question:** What can be inferred from the passage?
(A) The company is not profitable.
(B) The company's decisions suggest caution about the future despite current strong results.
(C) The company plans to close existing stores.
(D) The company's profits came from delaying store openings.

**Analysis:** A literal reading only confirms two separate facts: profits were high, and expansion was paused. Neither fact alone is the answer — (A) contradicts the passage outright, (C) and (D) invent facts never stated. The inference combines both facts: a profitable company pausing expansion signals it is being cautious about what comes next, which is precisely what (B) states without quoting any single sentence directly.

**Answer:** (B)

### (c) Tone/attitude question

**Passage excerpt:** "It is remarkable that, despite repeated warnings from engineers, the bridge's flawed design was approved without a single independent review."

**Question:** What is the author's attitude toward the approval of the bridge's design?
(A) Enthusiastic support
(B) Critical disapproval
(C) Complete indifference
(D) Cautious optimism

**Analysis:** The words "remarkable," "despite repeated warnings," and "without a single independent review" all carry negative judgment about how the decision was made — this is not neutral reporting of facts. (A) and (D) are positive, which contradicts the negative word choice; (C) implies no opinion at all, but the phrasing "it is remarkable that..." signals the author clearly does have one.

**Answer:** (B)

## 4. Timed Practice Set

**Passage (skim this once, then answer the questions below; suggested total time: 5 minutes for the full set):**

> Handwritten letters were once the primary way people stayed in touch across distances, but their use declined sharply after email and instant messaging became widely available. Despite this decline, a small but growing number of people have started writing letters again, describing the practice as a deliberate break from the speed of digital communication. Unlike a text message, a letter takes time to write, cannot be edited after it is sent, and arrives days later rather than instantly. Supporters argue that this slowness is exactly the point: it forces the writer to think carefully about what to say, and it gives the recipient something physical to keep, unlike a message that scrolls away and is quickly forgotten. Critics counter that letter-writing is impractical for most everyday communication, since urgent information — a change of plans, an emergency, a quick question — cannot wait several days for a reply. Even so, letter-writing enthusiasts do not claim it should replace digital communication entirely; they present it instead as a slower, more deliberate option for the kinds of messages where speed matters less than care.

1. What is the main idea of the passage? (40 sec)
2. What would letter-writing enthusiasts most likely say about using a letter to report a genuine emergency? (50 sec)
3. According to the passage, why do critics consider letter-writing impractical? (35 sec)
4. What is the author's tone toward the revival of letter-writing? (40 sec)
5. According to the passage, do letter-writing enthusiasts want letters to fully replace digital communication? (30 sec)

### Answer Key

1. **Letter-writing has declined but is being deliberately revived by some people as a slower alternative to digital communication.** This is the passage's overall shape: opening states the decline, the body explains the revival and its reasoning, and the closing sentence confirms letters are positioned as an alternative, not a replacement.
2. **They would likely agree a letter is not suitable for an emergency.** The passage never discusses this exact scenario, but two stated facts combine to support it: critics note that urgent information "cannot wait several days for a reply," and enthusiasts themselves frame letters as fitting only "messages where speed matters less than care" — an emergency is precisely a case where speed matters most, so the inference follows even though no sentence states it directly.
3. **Because urgent information cannot wait several days for a reply.** This is stated explicitly: "urgent information... cannot wait several days for a reply."
4. **Balanced/neutral.** The passage presents both the supporters' argument and the critics' counterargument without favoring either side or using judgmental language — it reports both positions evenly.
5. **No.** The passage explicitly states enthusiasts "do not claim it should replace digital communication entirely" and instead present it as a slower option for particular kinds of messages.
