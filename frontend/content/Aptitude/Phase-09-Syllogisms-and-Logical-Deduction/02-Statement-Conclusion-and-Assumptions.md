## 1. Concept

These questions give you a short **statement** (a fact, a policy, a piece of news, a notice, or an argument) and then test one of three different skills, which are easy to blur together but are actually testing different things:

- **Conclusion questions** ask what **necessarily follows** from the statement — a conclusion is a piece of information that *must* be true given the statement, derived the same way a syllogism conclusion is derived: no outside knowledge, no personal opinion, no "probably." A conclusion is essentially restating or directly deriving something the statement already logically forces.
- **Assumption questions** ask what the speaker/writer **must already believe** for the statement to make sense in the first place. An assumption is not stated anywhere in the text — it's the unstated ground the statement is standing on. If that ground weren't true, the statement would be pointless, illogical, or self-contradictory.
  - **Implicit assumptions** are the ones the exam is testing: they're necessary, unstated, and taken for granted by the speaker without needing to be said.
  - **Explicit assumptions** aren't really assumptions at all — if the text already says it directly, it's a stated fact, not an assumption. Don't pick an answer just because it "sounds reasonable" or "sounds true in general" — the test is whether the *statement itself* depends on it being true, not whether it's a nice-sounding belief.
- **Strong/weak argument questions** give a debatable question ("Should X be done?") plus one or more arguments for or against, and ask you to judge whether each argument is **strong** (directly relevant to the issue, factually grounded, addresses real consequences or causes) or **weak** (irrelevant, based on a superficial generalization, a vague slogan, or a minor/rare exception that doesn't actually address the core issue).
- **Course of action questions** give a problem and ask which of the proposed actions would be a **sensible, practical, and proportionate next step** to address it — as opposed to actions that are extreme, unrelated to the actual cause, or wildly disproportionate to the scale of the problem.

The one thread connecting all four: **you are never allowed to bring in outside opinions about what "should" happen in the real world.** You are only allowed to reason about what the given text logically requires, implies, or depends on.

## 2. Shortcut / Trick

**For conclusions:** ask, "Does this claim add any new information, or opinion, beyond what the statement already forces?" If a proposed conclusion is a direct restatement of a fact already given (even if reworded), it follows. If it generalizes ("all," "none," "always") beyond what the statement supports, or injects a value judgment ("this is good/bad," "people will be happy/unhappy"), it does not follow — these over-reaches are the most common wrong-answer trap.

**For assumptions — use the negation test.** Take the proposed assumption and flip it to its opposite. Ask: "If this negated version were true instead, would the original statement still make sense, still be logical, still serve its purpose?"
- If the statement **falls apart or becomes pointless** under the negation → the original assumption was necessary → it **is** an implicit assumption.
- If the statement **still holds up fine** even under the negation → the original assumption wasn't actually load-bearing → it is **not** an assumption (even if it sounds like a nice, true-in-general belief).

**For strong/weak arguments:** a strong argument must (a) directly address the specific issue in the question — not a generic, related-but-different topic — and (b) be grounded in a real, practical, or verifiable consequence, cause, or fact — not a vague appeal, an extreme worst-case, or a trivial exception. If an argument would apply equally well to almost any unrelated debate ("because it violates freedom" or "because some people like it"), that's a strong sign it's weak — it isn't engaging with what makes *this* issue specific.

**For course of action:** a good action must (a) target the actual cause or nature of the problem described, and (b) be proportionate in scale — matching the scope of the actual problem, not overreacting with a drastic, blanket, or irreversible measure when a targeted, investigative, or corrective step would do. "Investigate," "review," "issue an advisory," and "address the cause" are usually sensible; "ban entirely," "shut down indefinitely," "terminate everyone involved," and other extreme absolutes are usually not, unless the statement itself describes an emergency of matching scale.

## 3. Worked Examples

### (a) Statement + 2 conclusions, determine which follows

**Statement:** The government has decided to increase the retirement age of employees from 58 to 60 years.

**Conclusions:**
- I. Employees will now be able to work for two additional years before retiring.
- II. All employees will be happy with this decision.

**Checking I:** Raising the retirement age from 58 to 60 is, by definition, allowing/requiring two more years of service before mandatory retirement. This isn't new information or an inference beyond the statement — it's just restating what "increase from 58 to 60" already means. **Follows.**

**Checking II:** "Happy" is a value judgment about how people will feel, and the statement gives no information about employee sentiment at all — some employees might have wanted to retire at 58 and now can't, others might welcome working longer. Nothing forces this conclusion. **Does not follow.**

**Answer: Only conclusion I follows.**

### (b) Statement + assumption, determine if implicit

**Statement:** A notice pinned to the library door reads: "Silence please — exam in progress."

**Proposed assumption:** People passing by the library door are able to read and understand the language the notice is written in.

**Negation test:** Suppose this were false — suppose people passing by could *not* read the notice's language at all. Then the notice would fail to communicate anything to anyone; posting it would serve no purpose whatsoever. Since the act of posting a notice only makes sense if the writer believes it can be read and understood, this is something the writer necessarily takes for granted. **This is a valid implicit assumption.**

**Contrast — a proposed assumption that fails the test:** "Everyone who passes this door is a student." Negate it: suppose some passersby are not students (say, staff or visitors) — the notice still works exactly the same way; it still asks for silence, and nothing about it depends on the reader being a student specifically. Since the statement holds up fine under the negation, this is **not** an assumption — it's just an unrelated, unnecessary detail someone might guess at.

### (c) Course-of-action question

**Statement:** Absenteeism among the workers of a factory has increased considerably in recent months, significantly affecting production.

**Proposed courses of action:**
- I. The management should investigate the reasons behind the rise in absenteeism and address the underlying causes.
- II. The management should immediately terminate every worker who has been absent.

**Checking I:** This directly targets the actual problem described (rising absenteeism) at its source — finding out *why* it's happening before deciding what to fix. It's proportionate, practical, and matches the scale and nature of the issue. **Follows as a suitable course of action.**

**Checking II:** This is an extreme, blanket action that doesn't address why absenteeism is rising in the first place — and if absenteeism has genuine causes (illness, unsafe conditions, low morale), mass termination would likely worsen the production crisis by gutting the workforce rather than fixing it. It's disproportionate to what the statement describes. **Does not follow as a suitable course of action.**

**Answer: Only I is a suitable course of action.**

## 4. Timed Practice Set

1. Statement: Company X has announced a 20% bonus for all employees who complete their annual target by December. Conclusions: I. Employees who complete the target by December will receive a bonus. II. No employee will complete the target this year. (60 sec)
2. Statement: A notice on a laboratory door reads: "Authorized personnel only — biohazard materials inside." Assumption: People who read the notice understand what "biohazard" means and will heed the warning. Is this assumption implicit? (65 sec)
3. Question: Should fast-food advertisements targeting children be banned? Argument I: Yes, because children are more susceptible to persuasive advertising and are not yet able to critically evaluate such content. Argument II: No, because banning advertisements would violate freedom of speech in general. Which argument is strong? (70 sec)
4. Statement: There has been a sudden spike in reported cases of a viral infection in City X. Courses of action: I. The health department should investigate the outbreak and issue public health advisories for City X. II. All schools and public places across the entire country should be shut down indefinitely. Which is a suitable course of action? (70 sec)
5. Statement: Sales of smartphones priced above $800 have grown by 15% this quarter compared to last quarter. Conclusions: I. Some customers are willing to pay more than $800 for a smartphone. II. All customers prefer smartphones priced above $800. (60 sec)
6. Statement: The manager sent an email to all staff informing them that the office will remain closed on Friday for maintenance. Assumptions: I. Staff members have access to and check their email. II. All staff members will be pleased about the office closure. Which assumption is implicit? (65 sec)

### Answer Key

1. **Only I follows.** "20% bonus for employees who complete the target by December" directly restates as "employees who complete the target will receive a bonus" (I); "no employee will complete the target" (II) is an unsupported guess about future performance with no basis in the statement.
2. **Yes, implicit.** Negation test: if readers did not understand "biohazard" or ignored the warning, posting the notice would fail to serve its safety purpose — since the statement (posting a warning notice) only makes sense if the writer assumes it will be understood and heeded, this is a necessary, implicit assumption.
3. **Only Argument I is strong.** It directly engages with the specific issue (children's vulnerability to persuasive advertising) with a concrete, relevant reason; Argument II is weak because it's a generic slogan ("violates freedom of speech") that could be raised against almost any restriction and doesn't address why children specifically are or aren't at risk.
4. **Only I is suitable.** It targets the actual, localized problem (an outbreak in City X) with a proportionate, investigative response; II is a drastic, nationwide, indefinite action wildly disproportionate to a localized spike described in the statement.
5. **Only I follows.** A 15% quarter-on-quarter growth in sales above $800 directly implies such sales exist and are increasing, meaning at least some customers are paying that much (I); "all customers prefer" (II) is an unsupported universal generalization the statement never claims.
6. **Only I is implicit.** Negation test: if staff had no access to or never checked email, the notification would fail to reach anyone, defeating the purpose of sending it — so I is necessary. For II, negate it: even if some staff are unhappy about the closure, the email still successfully informs them of it — the statement's validity doesn't depend on staff being pleased, so II is not an assumption.
